Our approach

How learning works, and how we build on it

Five ideas from decades of research on memory and teaching. Every MindRise tool is built to respect them, and each chapter ends with something a student or an educator can try tomorrow.

Chapter I

Begin with attention

Learning starts before the material does. It starts with whether a student can actually look at it, free from the small interruptions that quietly spend the mind. A calm page is not a luxury; it is the first piece of teaching equipment.

Working memory is small. When an interface asks a student to decode itself, it borrows from the same account they need for the idea in front of them. We call that cognitive load, and we treat it as a budget.

For students: one task, one tab, phone out of reach for the length of a session. For educators: introduce one new idea at a time, and show a worked example before asking for independent work.

MindRise software never ranks students or counts points. Only understanding is worth measuring.
Chapter II

Retrieval over rereading

Rereading feels productive because the page looks familiar. Familiarity, however, is not knowledge. The dependable way to strengthen a memory is to pull it out of your head, which researchers call active recall, or the testing effect.

It asks the question first and shows the answer after. That small reversal is most of the method, and it works best when the stakes are low and feedback is quick.

For students: close the book and write down what you remember, then check. For educators: open a lesson with a few ungraded questions on last week’s material; the effort is the point, not the mark.

It feels harder than rereading. That is the sign that it is working.
Chapter III

Let time do some of the work

Memories fade quickly at first and then more slowly, and each time something is retrieved the fading slows. Reviewing at growing gaps, a practice known as spaced repetition, uses that to a student’s advantage. It is the opposite of cramming.

For students: a little every day beats a lot the night before. For educators: bring earlier units back in later homework and quizzes, so knowledge is revisited across the term, not stored away.

The curves here are schematic, not measurements from any one learner.
Chapter IV

Mix things up, deliberately

Practising one kind of problem over and over feels smooth and teaches less than it seems to. Mixing related topics, or interleaving, is slower and more effortful, and it trains the skill that exams and real problems actually demand: choosing the right idea.

Effort of this kind is what Robert Bjork named a desirable difficulty. Our tools add it carefully, and never so much that it becomes frustration.

For students: shuffle your practice questions instead of doing one topic at a time. For educators: set mixed problem sets, and expect them to feel harder than blocked ones; that is not a sign they are failing.

Difficulty is only desirable when it can be overcome.
Chapter V

Rest is part of the work

Memory is consolidated away from the desk: in pauses, on walks, and above all in sleep. A good study session ends while some energy is left, and a good tool lets a student stop without guilt.

That is why Cadence suggests breaks and never counts streaks, and why none of our products will send a reminder that makes a student feel behind. For educators, it is a reason to spread revision across the term rather than stacking it before an exam.
